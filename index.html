How much to trust these numbers

Read the SNR as good to about ±15 dB. Trends hold up better than the absolute level. The best bounce position, the benefit of a higher ceiling, and the fall-off with distance come out right because the uncertain terms move every case about equally. The forward-vs-backscatter comparison is less certain, because the two geometries lean on the cloud model in different ways.

Reading the comparison. The true answer most likely falls between the matte and droplet results. If backscatter loses under both models, it almost certainly loses in practice. If the two models disagree on the winner, only a field test will settle it.

Field results for comparison. Tasmanian amateurs reported a 209 km cloud-bounce contact on JT65 and a 288 km contact with a deeper weak-signal mode, using an array of 60 red LEDs behind 12 cm Fresnel lenses and a 10 mm APD receiver. Fed rough guesses for that station, the matte model puts the 209 km path near the JT65 decode limit, so it is in the right range. Too many of their parameters are unpublished to calibrate it more tightly.

Best calibration: one overcast-night test. Same-site backscatter (path distance 0, both units side by side) is the easiest way to measure your real cloud return at home.

Model

Geometry. Spherical Earth (R = 6371 km, no refraction). Forward mode puts the spot between A and B. Backscatter mode puts it past B on the same line, so both stations look the same way, A at a shallow angle and B more steeply. The spot can sit no farther out than A's horizon at the cloud base.

Scatter angle. The angle between the incoming beam and the ray to the receiver. Forward paths turn the light only a few degrees. Backscatter paths turn it almost 180°.

Matte surface. The cloud base is Lambertian: radiant intensity = Phit · ρ · cos θout / π. This ignores the scatter angle entirely.

Droplet scatter. Single scattering from a semi-infinite cloud with an approximate water-droplet phase function at 940 nm (reff ≈ 10 µm): intensity = Phit · P(Θ) · cos θout / (4π (cos θin + cos θout)). The phase function has a huge forward peak, a minimum near 100°, a rainbow near 138° and a small glory near 180°. Absorption is ignored.

Spot capture. If the receiver's field of view is narrower than the transmitter's spot, only the overlapping fraction counts. At grazing angles the spot stretches into a long ellipse. When the TX aim is below half its beamwidth, the lower part of the beam goes into terrain and the result is optimistic.

Atmosphere. Extinction falls off with height using the scale height above, integrated along each slant leg.

Noise. Detector NEP only. Plan on night operation.
